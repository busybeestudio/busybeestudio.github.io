How to Price Items for a Craft Fair (With a Worked Example)

Good craft fair prices cover four things: what the item cost you to make, the time you spent, your share of the booth costs, and a profit on top. Here is a simple way to work out each one, a full example with numbers, and how to make your prices easy for shoppers to read at a glance.

Step 1: Add up your materials

List everything that goes into one item: the main materials, plus the small things that are easy to forget, such as thread, glue, labels, tags and the bag or box it leaves in. If you buy in bulk, divide the price of the pack by how many items it makes.

Step 2: Pay yourself for your time

Time how long one item takes from start to finish, including finishing and packing. Then choose an hourly rate you would be happy to earn. Leaving your time out is the most common pricing mistake: it makes items look cheap to make when they are not, and it leaves nothing to grow on.

Step 3: Share out the booth costs

A fair has costs of its own: the booth fee, a table cover, signs, travel and card reader fees. Add up the costs for one event and divide by the number of items you expect to sell there. That gives each item its share.

Step 4: Add your profit and round the price

Add your costs together, then add a profit margin on top so the business can buy supplies ahead and cover a slow day. Round the result to a price that is easy to pay in cash, such as $5, $12 or $25. Round numbers also make change faster at a busy table.

If most shoppers pay by card, check what your card reader charges per sale and make sure the rounded price still covers it.

Check your price against the room

Before the fair, look at what similar handmade items sell for at local fairs and online. If your price is much higher, say why on your sign or tag (bigger size, natural materials, made to order). If it is much lower, you may be leaving money on the table, and very low prices can make shoppers wonder about quality.

Offer a spread of price points

Most booths sell better with a few small, easy buys (stickers, cards, ornaments) next to their main items and one or two bigger pieces. The small items pay for the booth on a quiet day, and the bigger pieces make the main items look like good value. A simple bundle, such as "2 for $25", can lift what each shopper spends.

Make every price easy to see

Shoppers often walk past a table where they cannot see prices, because many people do not like to ask. Put a clear tag or a small sign on every item, use the same style across the table, and keep the font large enough to read from a step away.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good profit margin for craft fair items?

There is no single right number. Many makers start with a margin on top of all their costs, including their time, then adjust after a fair or two based on what sold and what didn't. The key is that the price covers every cost first.

Should my craft fair prices match my online prices?

Keeping them close is fairer to your customers and simpler to manage. Online sales often have shipping and platform fees, while a fair has a booth fee, so work out both and choose prices that cover the higher one.

How do I price very small items?

Use the same steps, then round to an easy amount like $3 or $5. Small items are often priced in bundles (for example 3 for $10) so each sale is worth the time it takes.

Should I lower my prices at the end of the day?

It can move a few items, but shoppers who bought earlier may notice. A small bundle deal is usually a better way to clear stock than cutting single prices.
